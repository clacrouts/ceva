CEVA shoreline validation

Shoreline positions measured from Landsat and Sentinel-2, compared with beach surveys at six sites and with the published algorithms of the SDS benchmark. Every chart can be zoomed by dragging and shows the values under the pointer. Double-click a chart to reset it.

SummaryPublished algorithms

The CoastSat and CEVA solutions compared

Positions are metres along a cross-shore line, counted seaward. The error is satellite minus survey, so a negative bias means the satellite shoreline sits landward of the surveyed one. Bias is the mean error and spread its standard deviation; RMSE combines both. A constant bias does not affect rates of change; spread does.

Radar (Sentinel-1) as an additional source

Radar sees through clouds, so it adds waterlines where and when the imagery has none. Its waterlines are detected separately, then aligned on the imagery: for each transect and orbit direction, the offset between radar and the imagery positions within 30 days is removed, with terms in wave height and tide. Only imagery is used to align them, never the surveys. Yearly positions “imagery + radar” weight the two by their precision. The CEVA solution learns what the waterline looks like in radar from the imagery (a template of the radar profile across the imagery's waterline, per orbit direction and wave class) and finds it in every radar image by matching that template; the CoastSat solution uses CoastSat's own radar land/water model.

Against the published algorithms

The SDS benchmark (Vos et al. 2023) publishes the shoreline series each team produced. Here they are scored with the same surveys, transects and time matching as CEVA (survey interpolated to the image date when surveys exist within 10 days on both sides, otherwise the nearest survey within 3 days), so the numbers are directly comparable. Teams corrected for tides with the benchmark's recommended beach slope; most did not correct for waves.

Where the satellite waterline sits

For every satellite waterline matched to a survey, the height above the tide that the correction must use to put it exactly on the surveyed shoreline, with the beach slope CEVA uses. Within each beach it rises with the wave height by a similar amount; between beaches its mean differs. CEVA's “empirical” correction uses only the first part: a coefficient times the departure of the wave height from its usual value at the transect, fitted to all sites and tested on each site after fitting on the others only.

How the numbers were made

  • Imagery. Every Landsat 4 to 9 (Collection 2, tier 1) and Sentinel-2 scene over each site, 1984 to 2025. Clouds, shadows and snow are removed with the providers' flags (Sentinel-2: high-probability cloud and cirrus only, as CoastSat does).
  • CEVA solution. Surface reflectance read at 10 m steps along each transect. MNDWI = (green − SWIR1) / (green + SWIR1); the threshold is chosen with Otsu's method on the index samples near the expected shoreline, once per image and once per yearly composite, so it sits between the beach and water populations actually present.
  • CoastSat solution. Image chips around coastal segments of 3 km; CoastSat's neural network labels pixels sand, white water, water or other; Otsu separates the MNDWI of sand and water; the waterline is the 2D contour at that level, intersected with each transect. Sentinel-2 is read as top-of-atmosphere reflectance (Level-1C, what the classifier was trained on), Landsat as surface reflectance until top-of-atmosphere Landsat can be downloaded.
  • Radar (Sentinel-1, 2014 onwards). Terrain-corrected backscatter (gamma nought, VV and VH, 10 m) from the Planetary Computer, read like the imagery chips. Waterlines detected by each solution, then aligned on the imagery (see “Radar” above); they enter only the yearly positions marked “imagery + radar”.
  • Search window (both). Within 150 m of the expected waterline: that year's composite shoreline, moved with the tide of the image along a curve measured on composites of images grouped by tide, so that it follows the waterline across flat, macrotidal beaches.
  • Water level (both). Tide from the EOT20 model at each transect and image time, plus a wave correction fitted to the surveys: a coefficient per solution times the departure of the ERA5 significant wave height (about 20 km offshore) from its usual value at the transect.
  • Beach slope (both). The spectral method of Vos et al. (2020): the slope that leaves the least energy at the frequencies where the satellites alias the tide.
  • Yearly composite (after DEA Coastlines). For each year, the median index of the images taken with the tide in the central half of the observed range, then the seaward edge of the land.
  • Per image (after CoastSat). One waterline per image near that year's composite shoreline, moved to mean sea level with water level / tan β. Outliers beyond 3 scaled median deviations from a running median are dropped. The yearly value is the median of the remaining positions.
  • Survey shoreline. The 0 m contour (mean sea level; NAP in the Netherlands, AHD at Narrabeen) on each surveyed profile, found with the same seaward-edge rule.
  • Rates. Ordinary least squares on yearly positions, with a 95 % interval. Satellite and survey rates are fitted on the years both have.
  • Netherlands, survey-centred. JARKUS records the date of each profile. Satellite positions are also compared with the images taken around that date: the median of corrected single images within 45 days, and a composite of the images within 90 days.

What this validation does not cover

  • Six sandy and gravel coasts. Estuaries, cliffs, rocky and muddy coasts are untested. Duck (USA) and Torrey Pines (USA), the other benchmark sites, could not be added: one survey server is unreachable from this network and the other requires an account.
  • No co-registration between sensors.
  • Default settings were chosen on physical and published grounds, not tuned on these sites. The water-level cut-off of Castelle et al. (2021) is shown as a sensitivity curve, not applied.
